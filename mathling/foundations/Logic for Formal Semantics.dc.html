Arguments and logical form

Arguments and logical form
Corresponds to: the opening of Riemer Ch. 6 (validity, soundness, logical form)

Logic studies inference: which premises guarantee which conclusions. Semantics borrows from logic because the most stable part of a sentence's meaning, namely the conditions under which it is true, can be written down precisely in logical terms.

1.1Arguments, validity and soundness

Keep “reasoning correctly” apart from “saying what is true”.

Argument: a set of premises together with a conclusion.

Premise 1All humans are mortal.
Premise 2Socrates is a human.
Conclusion∴ Socrates is mortal.

Valid: if all the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. Validity depends only on the form of the inference, not on whether the premises are actually true.

Sound: the argument is valid and its premises are in fact all true. The conclusion of a sound argument is necessarily true.

Note: all three sentences in the bottom-left cell are true, yet the form of the inference does not guarantee the conclusion. Replace “human” with “dog” and you get true premises with a false conclusion.

1.2Logical form

Logical form: the skeleton left over once everything irrelevant to the inference has been stripped away. The skeleton of the “sound” cell above is:

All A are B; c is an A; ∴ c is a B

Whatever we substitute for A, B and c, the form stays valid, because validity is a property of form. Semantics goes a step further: natural-language sentences also have a logical form (not necessarily identical to their surface syntax), and it is this form that fixes their truth conditions. The second half of this guide answers the question of how to compute that logical form from a sentence.

1.3Entailment and other truth relations

Entailment (φ ⊨ ψ): every situation that makes φ true also makes ψ true. A valid argument is one where the conjunction of the premises entails the conclusion.

Example: “Ming bought a bicycle” ⊨ “Ming bought something”, but not the other way round. “Ming is in Taipei” and “Ming is in Kaohsiung right now” contradict each other.

Linguistic note

Entailment is not the only way one sentence can follow from another. “Ming quit smoking” lets us infer “Ming used to smoke”; negate it as “Ming didn't quit smoking” and the inference survives, so it is a presupposition, not an entailment. A genuine entailment disappears under negation: “Ming didn't buy a bicycle” does not imply “Ming bought something”. The negation test is the standard tool for telling the two apart.

Propositional logic

Propositional logic
Corresponds to: Riemer Ch. 6 (propositional logic, logic and natural language); C&C chapter on propositional logic

Propositional logic treats whole sentences as unanalysable atoms. It studies only how sentences combine with “not, and, or, if”, and how the truth value of the result is determined by its parts.

2.1Propositions and truth values

Proposition: content that can be true or false. Atomic propositions are written p, q, r, e.g. p = “Ming sings”, q = “Hua dances”.

Truth value: true is written 1, false 0. Questions and commands have no truth value and fall outside propositional logic.

Truth-functionality: the truth value of a compound proposition depends only on the truth values of its parts and on the connective. This is the most central assumption of propositional logic, and the one natural language challenges most often (see 2.5).

2.3Well-formed formulas, brackets and the main connective

Well-formed formulas are defined recursively:

  • every atomic proposition is a formula;
  • if φ is a formula, so is ¬φ;
  • if φ and ψ are formulas, so are (φ ∧ ψ), (φ ∨ ψ), (φ → ψ) and (φ ↔ ψ);
  • nothing else is a formula.

Brackets determine structure, and structure determines truth value. The connective at the root of the tree is the main connective; it determines what kind of formula the whole thing is. Truth values are computed layer by layer from the leaves to the root, which is what compositionality looks like in propositional logic.

2.4Tautology, contradiction and logical equivalence

Tautology: 1 in every row, e.g. p ∨ ¬p. Contradiction: 0 in every row, e.g. p ∧ ¬p. Contingency: true in some rows and false in others; most ordinary statements in natural language are of this kind.

Logical equivalence (φ ≡ ψ): the two formulas have the same value in every row, i.e. φ ↔ ψ is a tautology. The most useful ones:

¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q  ¬(p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q (De Morgan)
p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q ≡ ¬q → ¬p (contraposition)
¬¬p ≡ p  p ↔ q ≡ (p → q) ∧ (q → p)

Inference patterns: the validity of Chapter 1 can be checked with truth tables once we are in propositional logic. Two valid patterns and two common fallacies:

2.5Connectives and natural language

The central question of Riemer Ch. 6: can logic adequately represent natural-language meaning?

∧ and “and”
Logically, p ∧ q ≡ q ∧ p. Yet “She got married and had a baby” and “She had a baby and got married” sound different: coordination in natural language often carries temporal order or causation. “But” is also ∧; it only adds a sense of contrast that does not affect truth value.
∨ and “or”
∨ is inclusive: it is true when both disjuncts are true. “The set meal comes with soup or salad”, however, is often understood exclusively (only one of the two, written ⊕, with 0 in the first row). But in “If you have a driver's licence or a passport, you can enter”, having both is obviously fine. So the exclusive reading is a cancellable inference, not the literal meaning of “or”.
→ and “if”
When the antecedent is false, p → q is true: “If the moon is made of cheese, 2 + 2 = 4” is logically true even though the two halves have nothing to do with each other. Conversely, “If you mow the lawn, I'll give you five dollars” is often heard as if and only if (no mowing, no money); this is called conditional perfection.
¬ and “not”
¬ simply flips the truth value. Natural-language negation does more: Mandarin bù and méi distinguish volition or habit from completed events, and “The king of France is not bald” is more likely to deny the presupposition that there is a king of France (see 3.7).

Conversational implicature (Grice): meaning that the hearer infers on the assumption that the speaker is cooperative, but which is not part of the literal truth conditions. Grice held that in conversation we assume our partner provides enough information, truthfully and relevantly; on the basis of this assumption the hearer infers more than what is literally said.

Literal meaning
“Soup or salad” = soup ∨ salad: at least one of them. If both are included, the sentence is still true.
Implicature
Only one of the two. Reasoning: if the restaurant served both, it would simply have said “soup and salad”; since it only said “or”, presumably not both.

Defeasibility (also cancellability): the test that separates the two. An implicature can be cancelled by what follows without contradiction; literal meaning cannot:

✓It comes with soup or salad; in fact, you get both. merely adds information, cancelling “only one” ✗It comes with soup; in fact, there is no soup. contradiction: literal meaning can't be cancelled

“Only one” can be cancelled, so it is not part of the truth conditions of “or” but a pragmatic inference. This lets us analyse “or” uniformly as inclusive ∨ and leave the exclusive reading to pragmatics. The reasoning runs as follows:

The speaker said the weaker “p or q” ⇒ (had they known both were true, they would have said the stronger “p and q”)
⇒ the hearer infers: not both (scalar implicature)

There are two ways to respond to these gaps. One: logic is an idealization of natural-language meaning and was never meant to match it point by point. Two (the mainstream view since Grice): the semantics of the connectives is just their truth tables, and the extra temporal, exclusive or perfected readings are pragmatic inferences. Formal semantics takes the second route, which is why it can confidently represent meaning in terms of truth conditions.

Linguistic note

Mandarin splits logical ∨ into two words: huòzhě 或者 in declaratives and háishi 還是 in questions. Nǐ yào chá háishi kāfēi? ‘Do you want tea or coffee?’ is an alternative question and has no truth value; Nǐ yào chá huò kāfēi ma? is a yes/no question. The same logical operator can be divided into different words by sentence type in different languages.

Predicate logic

Predicate logic
Corresponds to: Riemer Ch. 6 (predicate logic, quantifiers, scope, definite descriptions, limits); C&C chapter on predicate logic

3.1What propositional logic cannot see

Translate the Socrates argument into propositional logic and the three sentences become three unrelated atoms: p, q ∴ r, which is an invalid form. The argument is valid because of what is inside the sentences, namely “all”, “human” and “mortal”, and propositional logic cannot see these parts. Predicate logic (also called first-order logic) analyses the inside of sentences, dealing with individuals, properties and quantification.

First-order: the quantifiers range only over individuals. The x in ∀x and ∃x ranges over the people, books and cats in the domain, but not over properties or relations.

OrderWhat quantifiers range overExample
Zerothno quantifiers (propositional logic)p ∧ q
Firstindividuals∃x(student(x) ∧ laugh(x)) Some student laughed
Secondproperties of and relations between individuals∃P(P(m) ∧ P(h)) Ming and Hua have something in common

“Ming has all of Hua's virtues” and “Most students laughed” both require talk about properties or sets, which goes beyond first-order logic (see 3.7). The typed λ-calculus of Chapter 6 allows expressions such as λP with variables over properties; it is a higher-order logic and can therefore handle these sentences.

3.2Individual constants, predicates and arguments

Individual constant: a symbol with a fixed referent, corresponding to a proper name. This guide uses m = Ming, h = Hua, s = Socrates.

Predicate: a property or relation with empty slots; the number of slots is its arity, and what fills a slot is an argument.

  • One-place: laugh(m) “Ming laughed”; human(s) “Socrates is human”
  • Two-place: like(m, h) “Ming likes Hua”
  • Three-place: give(m, h, b) “Ming gave Hua a book b”

Note: the order of arguments is a convention, but once fixed it must be used consistently: like(m, h) and like(h, m) are two different propositions.

3.3Variables and quantifiers

Variable: x, y, z, which, like pronouns, have no fixed referent. A formula with an unbound variable, such as laugh(x), is an open formula and has no truth value of its own.

Universal quantifier ∀x φ: φ holds for every individual x. Existential quantifier ∃x φ: there is at least one individual x for which φ holds.

Scope and binding: the formula immediately following a quantifier is its scope; variables of the same name within the scope are bound by it, those outside are free.

3.5Scope ambiguity

Scope ambiguity: with two quantifiers in one sentence, which one is outermost yields different truth conditions. Every student read a book has two readings:

(a)∀x(student(x) → ∃y(book(y) ∧ read(x, y))) ∀ > ∃: each their own (b)∃y(book(y) ∧ ∀x(student(x) → read(x, y))) ∃ > ∀: the same book

Quantifiers and negation also interact in scope: “Everyone didn't come” (∀ > ¬) and “Not everyone came” (¬ > ∀) have different truth conditions. Chapter 7 shows how scope ambiguity can be represented syntactically by quantifier raising.

Linguistic note

It is generally held (Huang 1982) that quantifier scope in Mandarin largely follows surface order: Yǒu yí ge xuésheng dú le měi yì běn shū ‘A student read every book’ normally has only the “same student” reading, unlike English A student read every book, which allows both. How freely scope can vary is itself an important topic in the study of cross-linguistic variation.

3.6Identity and definite descriptions

Identity (x = y): a special two-place predicate, true when both arguments denote the same individual. It is what lets us say “exactly one” or “exactly two”.

Definite description: “the F”. Russell's analysis breaks “The king of France is bald” into three parts:

∃x(KoF(x) ∧ ∀y(KoF(y) → y = x) ∧ bald(x))
existence: there is a king of France uniqueness: at most one predication: he is bald

On this analysis, since France has no king, the sentence is simply false; and “The king of France is not bald” has two scopes for negation: ¬ outside ∃ (“there is no such king at all”) or inside ∃ (“there is a king, and he is not bald”). Strawson objected that ordinary speakers judge the sentence neither true nor false, because its presupposition fails. This debate is one of the sources of the presupposition/entailment distinction discussed in 1.3.

3.7The limits of predicate logic

The following chapters fill these gaps.

“Most”
“Most students laughed” cannot be written with ∀, ∃ and the connectives alone. It compares the sizes of two sets: |student ∩ laugh| > |student − laugh|. The solution is to treat determiners as relations between sets, i.e. generalized quantifiers, which require higher types (Chapters 6–7).
Not word by word
“Every student” does not correspond to any continuous stretch of ∀x(student(x) → laugh(x)): ∀ and → are separated by “laugh”. To compose this formula step by step from the syntax, we need the λ-calculus (Chapter 6).
Non-intersective modifiers
“Grey cat” = grey(x) ∧ cat(x) works; but a “fake gun” is not a gun and a “former president” is not a president. Such adjectives are functions on properties, of type ⟨⟨e,t⟩,⟨e,t⟩⟩.
Intensional contexts
The Morning Star = the Evening Star, but “Ming thinks the Morning Star is a star” does not entail “Ming thinks the Evening Star is a star”. Extension (reference) is not enough; we also need intension (see 7.6). The same goes for tense and modality (“may”, “must”).

Models and truth conditions

Models and truth conditions
Corresponds to: Riemer Ch. 6 (truth conditions); C&C on the semantics of predicate logic and assignment functions

4.1Truth conditions

Truth conditions: the circumstances under which a sentence is true. Truth-conditional semantics holds that knowing the meaning of a sentence is, at least, knowing its truth conditions; one need not know whether it is actually true.

“Snow is white” is true if and only if snow is white. (Tarski's T-sentence)

On the left, in quotation marks, is the object language being talked about; on the right is the metalanguage used to describe the world. It looks like a tautology, but it states the task of semantics: to supply, systematically, the right-hand side for every sentence of the object language.

4.2Models

Model M = ⟨D, I⟩: D is the domain (the set of all individuals) and I is the interpretation function, which maps constants to individuals, one-place predicates to sets of individuals, and two-place predicates to sets of ordered pairs. We write ⟦α⟧M for the semantic value of α in M.

D= {Ming, Hua, Mei, book b} ⟦student⟧M= {Ming, Hua, Mei} ⟦laugh⟧M= {Ming, Hua} ⟦like⟧M= {⟨Ming, Hua⟩, ⟨Hua, Mei⟩}

So ⟦laugh(m)⟧M = 1, because Ming ∈ ⟦laugh⟧M; and ⟦like(h, m)⟧M = 0, because ⟨Hua, Ming⟩ is not in ⟦like⟧M. The same sentence may have a different truth value in a different model; a model is one way the world could be.

4.3Assignment functions and the semantics of quantifiers

Assignment function g: temporarily maps each variable to some individual in the domain, ⟦x⟧M,g = g(x). The truth value of an open formula is therefore relative to g.

Modified assignment g[x ↦ d]: exactly like g, except that x now points to d. What a quantifier does is “point x at each individual in turn, and check”:

⟦∀x φ⟧M,g = 1 iff for every d ∈ D, ⟦φ⟧M,g[x↦d] = 1
⟦∃x φ⟧M,g = 1 iff for at least one d ∈ D, ⟦φ⟧M,g[x↦d] = 1

Checking ∀x(student(x) → laugh(x)) in the model of 4.2:

x ↦student(x)laugh(x)student(x) → laugh(x)
Ming111
Hua111

One row is 0, so the universal sentence is false; Mei is a counterexample. Look at the book b row: the antecedent is false, so the conditional is true. This is exactly why ∀ must go with →: individuals that are not students should not affect a judgement about “every student”.

4.4The principle of compositionality

Principle of compositionality (usually attributed to Frege): the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined.

It explains how we can understand sentences we have never heard: the vocabulary is finite and so are the rules of combination, yet the sentences are infinite. The semantics of propositional logic (2.3) and predicate logic are both compositional; the goal of formal semantics is to make natural language itself work this way too, with one semantic value per word and one composition rule per syntactic configuration. The materials needed for this are sets, functions and types.

Linguistic note

Idioms such as Mandarin chī cù 吃醋 ‘be jealous’ (lit. ‘eat vinegar’) or English kick the bucket are the classic challenge to compositionality: the meaning of the whole cannot be derived from the literal parts. The usual move is to list them in the lexicon as unanalysable units; the principle of compositionality applies only to free combinations.

Chapter 5

Sets, relations and functions

Sets, relations and functions
Corresponds to: C&C chapter on sets, relations and functions

The semantic values of formal semantics are not vague “concepts” but concrete objects of set theory: individuals, truth values, sets and, most importantly, functions.

5.1Sets

Set: an abstract collection determined solely by who its members are, regardless of order or repetition. a ∈ A: a is a member of A; A ⊆ B: every member of A is in B; ∅: the empty set.

Set-builder notation: {x | laugh(x)} reads “the set of all x such that laugh(x) is true”. It and the λ-term λx.laugh(x) are two ways of writing the same idea (see 5.4).

5.2Ordered pairs and relations

Ordered pair ⟨a, b⟩: order matters, ⟨a, b⟩ ≠ ⟨b, a⟩ (unless a = b). A × B is the set of all ⟨a, b⟩ with a ∈ A and b ∈ B.

Relation: any subset of A × B. The ⟦like⟧ = {⟨Ming, Hua⟩, ⟨Hua, Mei⟩} of 4.2 is a relation on D × D.

5.3Functions

Function f : A → B: a special kind of relation in which every member of the domain A is paired with exactly one value in B, written f(a).

5.4Characteristic functions: sets as functions

Characteristic function: the characteristic function of a set S ⊆ D is fS : D → {0, 1}, mapping members to 1 and non-members to 0. Sets and their characteristic functions correspond one to one and can be used interchangeably.

This shift matters a great deal: once the semantic value of a predicate is viewed as a function, “laugh(m)” is no longer “check membership” but function application: apply ⟦laugh⟧ to Ming and get 1. The entire compositional machinery of formal semantics rests on this shift.

5.5Currying: one argument at a time

Currying (also Schönfinkelization): rewrite “a function of two arguments” as “a function that takes one argument and returns another function”. Two-place “like” first takes the object, yielding a one-place predicate “like someone”, and then takes the subject:

Basic types: e (entity), with domain De = D; t (truth value), with Dt = {0, 1}.

6.3λ-abstraction: naming functions

First why we need it, then how to write it.

Chapter 5 said that the semantic values of predicates are functions, and FA in Chapter 7 requires every node to be a function or an argument. The problem is that predicate logic has no way to write down these functions. The λ-operator solves the following four difficulties.

1. Tables never end
In 5.5 we could draw ⟦like⟧ as a nested table only because the domain had three people. With a large or infinite domain the table can never be finished. More fundamentally, word meanings should not be tied to a particular model: in every model, “laugh” is “the function that maps those who laugh to 1”. We need a notation that states the rule without listing the members.
2. Open formulas aren't functions
The closest thing to a “property” in predicate logic is an open formula like like(x, h). But its type is t: under an assignment g it yields a truth value that varies with g(x). It is not a function of type ⟨e,t⟩ and cannot combine with a subject by FA. λx binds the free x, turning “a truth value that depends on x” into “a function that takes x as input”.
3. Words don't match pieces
As noted in 3.7, in ∀x(student(x) → laugh(x)) “every” corresponds to ∀, → and two empty slots, scattered across the whole formula. With predicate logic alone we can only translate whole sentences and cannot give “every” a meaning of its own. λP.λQ.∀x(P(x) → Q(x)) uses λ to mark the two slots to be filled, so a lexical item can enter composition with its slots open.
4. Language abstracts too
“(the person) Zhangsan likes” hollows out one position in the sentence “Zhangsan likes ＿” to build a property. Quantifier raising (7.5) and relative clauses (9.7) perform the same operation, and λ is its counterpart in logic: λx.like(z, x).

Intuitively, λ is a fill-in-the-blank question: λx announces “there is a blank here, call it x”, and what follows the dot is the question itself.

Fill in the blankλ-termType

What goes in the blank determines the first half of the type; what you get once it is filled determines the second half. Filling in the blank is β-reduction (6.4 below). Where a maths class writes f(x) = x² + 1, λ-notation writes λx.x² + 1: the function does not need a name f first and can be used directly as a semantic value.

λ-term λx.φ: “the function that maps any x to the value of φ”. With it we can write down any function without drawing the whole table.

Typing rules: in the typed λ-calculus every expression carries a type, and only expressions whose types match are well formed.

Abstraction: if x is of type σ and φ of type τ, then λx.φ is of type ⟨σ, τ⟩

7.2Five composition rules

Terminal Nodes TN
If α is a terminal node, ⟦α⟧ is given by the lexicon.
Non-branching Nodes NN
If α has a single daughter β, then ⟦α⟧ = ⟦β⟧.
Functional Application FA
If α has daughters β and γ, ⟦β⟧ of type ⟨σ, τ⟩ and ⟦γ⟧ of type σ, then ⟦α⟧ = ⟦β⟧(⟦γ⟧). Order doesn't matter.
Predicate Modification PM
If α has daughters β and γ, both of type ⟨e, t⟩, then ⟦α⟧ = λx.⟦β⟧(x) ∧ ⟦γ⟧(x).
Predicate Abstraction PA
If α has daughters index i and β, then ⟦α⟧g = λx.⟦β⟧g[i↦x]. Traces and pronouns: ⟦ti⟧g = g(i).

FA does most of the work; PM handles two properties side by side (“grey cat”); PA turns a sentence back into a property after a quantifier has moved (7.5). These five rules plus a lexicon make up a small semantic fragment of the language.

8.1Lexical decomposition

Predicative decomposition: break verb meanings down into a few recurring semantic primitives, such as CAUSE and BECOME. Capitals mark them as metalanguage constants, not English words.

BECOME⟨t,t⟩ “go from not-φ to φ” CAUSE⟨t,⟨e,t⟩⟩ “x brings about φ” ⟦openintr⟧ = λy.BECOME(open(y))⟨e,t⟩ ⟦opentr⟧ = λy.λx.CAUSE(BECOME(open(y)))(x)⟨e,⟨e,t⟩⟩

Note: CAUSE(φ)(x) is abbreviated below as CAUSE(x, φ). The causative/inchoative alternation (“Ming opened the door” / “The door opened”) thus shares a single core, BECOME(open(y)).

Exercise 1 How decomposition predicts entailments

(a) Using the rules of 7.2, derive “Ming opened the door” (⟦the door⟧ = d; ignore tense). (b) Explain why “Ming opened the door” ⊨ “The door opened”, and why “Ming opened the door, but the door didn't open” is a contradiction.

Answer
⟦VP⟧ = [λy.λx.CAUSE(x, BECOME(open(y)))](d)FA ⇒ λx.CAUSE(x, BECOME(open(d)))β ⟦S⟧ ⇒ CAUSE(m, BECOME(open(d)))FA, β

(b) Add a meaning postulate: CAUSE(x, φ) ⊨ φ (what is brought about actually happens). Then ⟦S⟧ ⊨ BECOME(open(d)) = ⟦the door opened⟧. Entailments need not be listed pair by pair; they follow directly from the structure of word meaning, which is the main benefit of decomposition. The second clause, ¬BECOME(open(d)), conflicts with this, so the whole sentence is a contradiction (2.4).
Exercise 2 Does “kill” mean “cause to die”?

Suppose ⟦kill⟧ = λy.λx.CAUSE(x, BECOME(¬alive(y))). Compare “Ming poisoned the drink on Saturday and caused Hua to die on Sunday” with “?Ming killed Hua on Sunday (the poison was given on Saturday)”. What challenge does this pose for decomposition?

Answer

This is Fodor's (1970) famous objection to decomposition: the periphrastic version lets the cause and the result be modified as two separate events, while lexical “kill” offers only one event for a temporal adverb to modify. There are two replies: (i) lexical CAUSE expresses direct causation, stricter than “cause to”; (ii) decompositions are metalanguage structures and do not claim to be synonymous with any natural-language phrase. The Lexicon takes the second line: decomposition is meant to explain entailments and argument realization, not to paraphrase.
Linguistic note

Mandarin offers direct evidence for decomposition. It is generally held (Tai 1984) that shā 殺 ‘kill’ on its own denotes only the action and does not entail the result: shā le bàntiān méi shā sǐ ‘tried to kill for a long time but didn't kill (him) dead’ is acceptable; the result must be made explicit by the complement of the resultative compound shā sǐ ‘kill-dead’. The CAUSE + BECOME(¬alive) that English packs into one word, Mandarin spreads over two words in the syntax.

8.2Fine-grained types and selectional restrictions

Chapter 6 had only e and t. But “drink” does not accept just any individual: its object must be a liquid and its subject animate. Lexical semantics subdivides e into a type hierarchy, so that selectional restrictions become type checking.

⟦drink⟧ = λy : liquid . λx : animate . drink(x, y)

Note: λy : σ reads “accepts only a y of type σ”. A subtype can fill the position of its supertype (human ⊑ animate), but not vice versa.

Exercise 3 Type checking

Decide whether each sentence passes type checking, and say which λ it gets stuck on: (a) Ming drank coffee. (b) #Ming drank a rock. (c) #The rock drank coffee. (d) This car really drinks gas.

Answer

(a) coffee : liquid ✓, Ming : human ⊑ animate ✓. (b) rock : solid, stuck at λy : liquid. (c) a rock is not animate, stuck at λx : animate. (b) and (c) are not false but semantically anomalous: with mismatched types no truth value can be computed (a lexical version of the third row of 6.2). (d) formally also gets stuck at λx, yet is perfectly acceptable: the car is construed as an animate consumer. Type requirements can be “adjusted” by context; this is the problem type coercion (8.4) deals with.

8.3Qualia structure

Qualia structure: the meaning of a noun is not just a set; it has four dimensions: FORMAL (what it is), CONST (what it is made of), TELIC (what it is for) and AGENTIVE (how it comes into being).

book
FORMALphysical • information CONSTpages, chapters TELICread(e, y, x) AGENTIVEwrite(e′, z, x)
coffee
FORMALliquid CONSTbeans, water TELICdrink(e, y, x) AGENTIVEbrew(e′, z, x)

TELIC and AGENTIVE are events (e and e′ are event variables). A noun therefore carries several events of its own, which a verb can retrieve when it needs an event; that is the topic of the next page.

8.4Type coercion

Type coercion: when a function requires type σ but its argument is of type τ, instead of ruling the combination anomalous we retrieve a value of type σ from the argument's meaning (usually from its qualia structure) and substitute it. It is a third way out of the “type mismatch” of 6.2 (the other two being PM and QR).

⟦enjoy⟧ = λe : ε . λx : animate . enjoy(x, e) wants an event

9.3Hěn and adjectival de

In affirmative declaratives, an adjective almost always needs hěn ‘very’ in front of it (Zhāngsān hěn cōngming ‘Zhangsan is smart’), but not in negatives, questions or comparatives. A simple treatment is to take hěn as a semantically empty identity function:

⟦hěn⟧ = λf⟨e,t⟩.f ⇒ ⟦Zhangsan hěn cōngming⟧ = smart(z)

B: a rule

9.5Definite nà: partial functions

Mandarin has no definite article; definiteness is often expressed by the demonstratives nà ‘that’ / zhè ‘this’. If the department building has two staircases, east and west, saying xìguǎn nà ge lóutī huài le ‘that staircase in the building is broken’ leaves the hearer unable to tell which; if the building has no lift at all, ‘that lift in the building is broken’ refers to nothing. In both cases nà fails to find a unique object.

⟦nà⟧ = λf⟨e,t⟩ : there is exactly one x in context C with f(x) = 1 . ιy[f(y) = 1]
type ⟨⟨e,t⟩,e⟩; ιy reads “the unique y”

Partial function: a function defined on only part of its domain. The condition between the colon and the dot is the domain restriction of nà: when it is not met there is no output, and the phrase and sentence containing it go uninterpreted as well.

This agrees with Strawson's side in 3.6: “The king of France is bald” is not false but lacks a truth value; the presupposition is written here as a domain condition on a function. And although there are countless students in the world, we can still say “that student”, because uniqueness is only required within the contextually relevant set C.

If there is no book in the context, or more than one relevant book, the domain condition of ⟦nà⟧ is not met, the DP has no value, and by interpretability (9.2) the sentence has no truth value either, rather than being false.

9.6Pronouns and assignment functions

⟦·⟧ is a function, so one input cannot have two outputs; yet tā ‘he’ refers to Wang Xiaoming this time and to Lisi the next. The solution is to make semantic values relative to a contextual assignment function g (4.3): pronouns carry indices, and g maps indices to individuals.

⟦tān⟧g = g(n)  non-pronouns: ⟦α⟧g = ⟦α⟧

Mandarin relative clauses have a gap but no relative pronoun like who/which, only de. Two analyses:

Null operator
A silent operator moves to the front of the clause, coindexed with the gap: [Op1 [Zhangsan xǐhuan e1 de]] (Huang 1982). De is a semantically empty marker; Op1 triggers PA (7.2).
de as λ
An indexed de binds the gap directly: ⟦[α den]⟧g = λx.⟦α⟧g[n↦x], a Mandarin version of the PA rule.
⟦Zhangsan xǐhuan e1 de⟧ = λx.like(z, x)
→ PM → ⟦… de rén⟧ = λx.like(z, x) ∧ person(x)

9.8A unified semantics for de: the covert relation R

But de occurs far beyond gapped relative clauses. Zhāngsān de shū ‘Zhangsan's book’, shù shang de shé ‘the snake in the tree’ and tā tán gāngqín de shēngyīn ‘the sound of him playing the piano’ have no gap; if de were empty, ⟦Zhangsan de⟧ = z would combine with ⟦shū⟧ to give something like “Zhangsan is a book”, which is plainly wrong. Jo-wang Lin's proposal: de introduces a contextually determined relation R between modifier and head, and the modifier may be of any type.

⟦de⟧ = λα.λP⟨e,t⟩.λy.R(y)(α) ∧ P(y) α of any type
Noun phraseType of αValue of R in context
Zhāngsān de shū ‘Zhangsan's book’eowns, wrote, bought…
zuótiān de huìyì ‘yesterday's meeting’timeis the time at which … took place
shù shang de shé ‘the snake in the tree’placeis the location of …
zìsī de rén ‘selfish people’⟨e,t⟩(property) holds of …
tā tán gāngqín de shēngyīn ‘the sound of him playing the piano’tis the source/cause of …
Lǐsì pòchǎn de xiāoxi ‘the news that Lisi went bankrupt’tidentity: is the content of the news

R is a free variable, valued by the assignment function g like the pronouns of 9.6; its possible values are constrained by encyclopaedic knowledge about the head noun, e.g. books are written, bought and sold, and owned. This echoes the qualia structure of 8.3: the preference of ‘Zhangsan's book’ for an AGENTIVE (wrote) or possession reading is the noun's meaning constraining R.

Exercise 10 Zhāngsān xǐhuan nà yí wèi jiāo shùxué de lǎoshī ‘Zhangsan likes that teacher who teaches maths’

Combining 9.4, 9.5 and 9.7, compute the truth conditions of the sentence.

Answer
⟦[e1 jiāo shùxué] de⟧ = λx.teach(x, maths)PA ⟦jiāo shùxué de lǎoshī⟧ = λx.teach(x, maths) ∧ teacher(x)PM ⟦yí wèi … lǎoshī⟧ = λx.teach(x, maths) ∧ teacher(x) ∧ OUwèi(x) = 1classifier ⟦nà …⟧ = ιy[teach(y, maths) ∧ teacher(y) ∧ OUwèi(y) = 1] call it t*⟦nà⟧ ⟦S⟧ = like(z, t*)FA ×2

Presupposition: there is exactly one maths teacher in the context. The classifier applies outside ‘teacher who teaches maths’, following the surface order nà yí wèi [jiāo shùxué de lǎoshī]; with the order [jiāo shùxué de] nà yí wèi lǎoshī, the same rules applied in a different order give the same result, which can be used to discuss the position of Mandarin relative clauses.
Linguistic note

The research part of the same project deals with temporal interpretation in Mandarin. Lin (2010, Linguistic Inquiry) argues that Mandarin is a tenseless language: sentences have no syntactic tense, and temporal reference is determined jointly by time adverbials, aspect markers (le, guo, zài) and pragmatic reasoning. The claim in 7.6 that tense is handled along the same route therefore needs rethinking for Mandarin.
