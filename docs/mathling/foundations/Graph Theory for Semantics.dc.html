PRELIMINARIES TO SEMANTICS · AN ILLUSTRATED PRIMER

Graph Theory for Semantics

From graphs, trees and lattices to the Chinese Wordnet

Shu-Kai Hsieh, editor*

Logic tells us when a sentence is true; graph theory describes how words and word senses are connected. This guide starts from the basic definitions of graphs, moves through trees, directed acyclic graphs, partial orders and lattices, and centrality, reviews semantic networks and knowledge graphs, and ends with WordNet and the Chinese Wordnet (CWN): how to measure sense similarity, how to use graphs for word sense disambiguation, and how to turn graphs into vectors. The last chapter includes a small wordnet browser you can operate.

Chapter 1

Basic concepts of graphs

Graphs: vertices and edges
Sources: standard definitions from introductory graph theory

Language is full of relations: synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, part and whole, co-occurrence, collocation. Graph theory offers a neutral vocabulary for describing them: it does not ask what a relation means, only who is connected to whom, how, and how tightly.

1.1Graphs: nodes and edges

Graph G = (V, E): V is the set of nodes (vertex, node), and E is the set of edges. An edge of an undirected graph is an unordered pair {u, v}, written u–v.

In the figure, V = {glad, happy, cheerful, pleasant, blessed, joyful}, and E has 6 edges. A graph does not care where its nodes are drawn: as long as the connections are the same, every layout is the same graph.

1.2Adjacency, degree and the handshaking lemma

Adjacent: two nodes joined by an edge are neighbours. Degree deg(v): the number of edges incident to v. A node of degree 0 is an isolated node.

Handshaking lemma: every edge contributes exactly two to the total degree, so

Σv∈V deg(v) = 2|E|　　Above: 2 + 4 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 12 = 2 × 6

Note: it follows that the number of odd-degree nodes is always even. Simple counts like this are the first check for errors in graph data.

A lexical network usually has tens or hundreds of thousands of nodes, yet each word is linked to only a few others, so the matrix is almost all zeros (a sparse graph). In practice adjacency lists are used; matrices come into play when linear algebra is needed (Chapters 5 and 12).

1.4Labelled and weighted graphs

Labelled graph: edges carry type labels. An edge in a semantic network says not only that two nodes are connected but which relation holds: hypernymy, meronymy, synonymy … Formally, an edge becomes a triple:

E ⊆ V × L × V　　e.g. ⟨dog, hypernym, mammal⟩, ⟨tail, part, dog⟩

The same pair of nodes may be joined by several edges with different labels; the RDF triples of Chapter 7 have exactly this form.

Weighted graph: each edge carries a number w(u, v), such as the co-occurrence count of two words in a corpus, or the similarity of two senses. Weights make “how tightly connected” measurable (Chapters 3 and 10).

Linguistic lens

What the nodes of a lexical network are determines what the graph looks like. If the nodes are word forms, a polysemous word ties unrelated meanings together: Chinese 打 dǎ links to the neighbours of 打電話 ‘make a call’, 打球 ‘play ball’ and 打毛衣 ‘knit a sweater’ at once, becoming a spurious hub. If the nodes are senses (as in WordNet and CWN), 打₁ and 打₂ are separate nodes, and the graph reflects meaning rather than spelling. Chapters 8 and 9 return to this distinction.

Chapter 2

Digraphs, trees and DAGs

Digraphs, trees and DAGs
Sources: standard definitions from introductory graph theory

2.1Directed graphs

Directed graph: edges are ordered pairs (u, v), written u → v. For v, the in-degree deg−(v) of v is the number of edges pointing to it; the out-degree deg+(v) is the number of edges leaving it.

Synonymy is symmetric and suits undirected edges; hypernymy has a direction: “a dog is a kind of animal” is not “an animal is a kind of dog”. Every directed semantic relation has an inverse: hypernym/hyponym, holonym/meronym. Databases usually store only one direction and derive the other in code (this is what reversed means in CwnGraph, Chapter 9).

2.2Trees

Tree: a connected undirected graph with no cycles. A tree with n nodes has exactly n − 1 edges, and there is exactly one path between any two nodes.

Rooted tree: one node is designated the root, and all edges are oriented towards (or away from) it. This gives parents, children, ancestors, descendants and leaves (nodes with no children). The depth of a node is its distance from the root; the height of the tree is the maximum depth.

A taxonomy compresses “is a kind of” knowledge into structure: to find out whether a dog is an organism, just follow the arrows upward and see whether you pass “organism”. This upward walk is the transitivity and inheritance of Chapter 4.

Graph Theory for SemanticsDigraphs and trees

2.3DAGs and multiple inheritance

Cycle: a path that starts at a node, follows edges and returns to it. Directed acyclic graph: a directed graph with no directed cycle.

Hypernymy cannot contain a cycle: dog → animal → … → dog would say that a dog is a subordinate kind of dog. But hypernymy need not be a tree either: a concept can have two hypernyms.

This is called multiple inheritance. In WordNet a few noun synsets do have two hypernyms; CWN often handles words like “book”, which have both a physical and a content side, with facets (Chapter 9).

Topological sort: the nodes of a DAG can be lined up so that every edge points forward. A graph with a cycle cannot be, so topological sorting doubles as a cycle detection tool: when building a wordnet, it checks for mistakenly entered circular hypernym links.

e.g.The most common method is Kahn’s algorithm: repeatedly take a node that no edge points to, append it to the sequence, and delete its outgoing edges. In the “book” graph, only “book” is initially unpointed-to. ① Take book; artifact and information are no longer pointed to. ② Take artifact; object is no longer pointed to. ③ Take information; abstraction is no longer pointed to. ④ object. ⑤ abstraction. The result is book, artifact, information, object, abstraction, as in figure (a). The order is not unique: book, information, abstraction, artifact, object is also valid.
If “object → book” is entered by mistake, even “book” has an incoming edge; all five nodes are pointed to and the algorithm cannot take its first step. In general, if the algorithm stops with nodes left unplaced, the remaining nodes must contain a cycle (here book → artifact → object → book). That is how cycle detection works.

Exercise 1　Draw the taxonomy

The following are hypernym links (left is a kind of right): rose→flower, flower→plant, pine→tree, tree→plant, plant→organism, herb→plant, mint→herb, mint→spice. (a) Draw the graph. (b) Is it a tree? (c) List all leaves and all ancestors of “mint”.

Answer
(a) Put “organism” at the top and connect downward. (b) No: “mint” has two parents (herb, spice), and “spice” is not connected to “organism”, so the graph has two top nodes (organism, spice); it is a DAG. (c) Leaves: rose, pine, mint. Ancestors of mint: herb, plant, organism, spice.
Incidentally, to give the whole graph a unique root, add a common top node (e.g. “entity”) to which the hypernym chains of both “organism” and “spice” lead; entity plays this role in the WordNet noun hierarchy.

Paths, distance and shortest paths

Paths, distance and shortest paths
Sources: standard definitions from introductory graph theory and algorithms

3.1Paths and connectivity

Path: a sequence of nodes v0, v1, …, vk, with an edge between each consecutive pair and no node repeated. The length of the path is the number of edges, k.

Connected: a graph with a path between any two nodes. A disconnected graph splits into connected components, each internally connected and disjoint from the others.

Draw the synonym graph of a language and you usually get one large connected component and many small islands; the islands tend to be highly specialised terms. The size distribution of components is a simple index for comparing the coverage of two lexical resources.

3.2Distance and breadth-first search

Distance d(u, v): the length of the shortest path from u to v; ∞ if they are disconnected.

Breadth-first search: starting from a source, visit all nodes at distance 1, then all at distance 2, and so on. Implemented with a queue, each node enters the queue once, and the layer at which it is first visited is its distance from the source.

3.3Weighted shortest paths

Dijkstra’s algorithm: when edge weights are non-negative, repeatedly take the unsettled node with the smallest accumulated distance, settle its distance, and update its neighbours.

In a lexical network, weights can come from a corpus (the rarer the co-occurrence, the greater the distance) or be set by relation type (synonym 0, hypernym 1, similar 2). Different weighting schemes give different “nearest words”.

3.4Diameter, average path length and small worlds

Diameter: the largest distance over all pairs of nodes. Average path length L: the mean distance over all pairs.

Small-world network (Watts & Strogatz 1998): many nodes, yet a short average path length (roughly proportional to log n), together with a high proportion of neighbours that are linked to each other (the clustering coefficient of Chapter 5).

e.g.In the left graph, each node is linked only to two neighbours on each side; reaching the opposite node takes 4 steps, and the average path length is L = 2.40; but most neighbours are linked to each other, giving a clustering coefficient C = 0.50. Adding a single shortcut between opposite nodes brings L down to 2.21. The middle graph rewires just 3 of its 32 edges: L falls to 2.07 while C stays at 0.36. The right graph rewires every edge at random: L = 1.96, but C drops to 0.21. In a lexical network, polysemous words act as shortcuts: Chinese 打 dǎ connects to telephones and communication on one side and to balls and sport on the other, joining two otherwise distant semantic fields.

Linguistic lens

Sigman & Cecchi (2002) analysed the WordNet noun network and found that with hypernymy alone it is a very deep tree with long average paths; once the different senses of polysemous words are linked, the average path length shrinks sharply and the whole lexicon becomes a small world. Polysemy works like a transit hub, joining distant semantic fields. Steyvers & Tenenbaum (2005) observed similar small-world structure in word-association data, WordNet and Roget’s Thesaurus.

Partial orders, lattices and inheritance

Partial orders, lattices and inheritance
Sources: introductory discrete mathematics; on the transitivity of meronymy see Cruse (1986)

4.1Four properties of binary relations

Chapter 1 drew relations as edges; this chapter turns the edges back into a set-theoretic binary relation R ⊆ V × V and asks which properties it has.

PropertyDefinitionExample
reflexivefor all x, x R x“is a kind of (including itself)”
symmetricx R y ⇒ y R xsynonymy, antonymy, similarity
antisymmetricx R y and y R x ⇒ x = yhypernymy
transitivex R y and y R z ⇒ x R zhypernymy: dog → mammal → animal

Semantic relations do not always have the ideal properties. Near-synonymy is symmetric but not transitive: “hot” is close to “warm”, “warm” is close to “cool”, yet “hot” is not close to “cool”. The transitivity of meronymy is also disputed: a handle is part of a door and a door is part of a house, but “the handle is part of the house” sounds odd (Cruse 1986).

4.2Partial orders and Hasse diagrams

Partial order ≤: a reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive relation. It is “partial” because not every two elements are comparable: neither dog nor rose is a kind of the other.

Transitive closure: a wordnet stores only direct hypernym edges (dog → mammal). Adding every edge derivable along paths, plus reflexivity, gives the hypernym order “x ≤ y: x is a kind of y”.

e.g.Let the direct hypernym edges be dog → mammal, cat → mammal and mammal → animal: 3 in all. The transitive closure adds dog ≤ animal and cat ≤ animal, plus four reflexive pairs x ≤ x, for 9 pairs in total. Check each property: reflexive, every word is a kind of itself; transitive, dog ≤ mammal and mammal ≤ animal give dog ≤ animal; antisymmetric, if x ≤ y and y ≤ x, then x = y. Antisymmetry explains why a wordnet puts synonyms into a single node: if “computer” and “computing machine” were separate nodes, each the hypernym of the other, antisymmetry would fail. Finally, neither dog ≤ cat nor cat ≤ dog: they are an incomparable pair, which is what “partial” means.

The hypernym order runs in the same direction as logical entailment: “dog ≤ animal” corresponds to ∀x(dog(x) → animal(x)), so “Amin keeps a dog” ⊨ “Amin keeps an animal”. (See Logic for Formal Semantics 1.3.)

4.3Upper bounds, lowest common subsumers and lattices

Upper bound: c is ≥ both a and b. Least upper bound a ∨ b: the lowest of all upper bounds. In a wordnet it is the lowest common subsumer (lowest common subsumer, LCS), the core of every similarity formula in Chapter 10.

Greatest lower bound a ∧ b: the highest element that is ≤ both a and b. Lattice: a partial order in which every two elements have a ∨ and a ∧.

e.g.In the figure, the upper bounds of dog are {dog, mammal, animal} and those of cat are {cat, mammal, animal}; the common upper bounds are {mammal, animal}, of which the lowest is mammal, so dog ∨ cat = mammal. Likewise dog and penguin share only {animal}, so dog ∨ penguin = animal. Comparable elements are the simplest case: ∨ is the higher one and ∧ the lower, e.g. dog ∨ mammal = mammal and dog ∧ mammal = dog.

A taxonomy is not a lattice: dog and cat have no common hyponym, so ∧ does not exist. Multiple inheritance can also make ∨ non-unique: if “book” and “newspaper” are both hyponyms of “artifact” and “information”, both are minimal common upper bounds, and similarity computation needs a tie-breaking rule (usually the deepest or most informative one).

e.g.The common upper bounds of book and newspaper are artifact, information and entity. Entity is above the other two and is not lowest; neither artifact nor information has a lower common upper bound beneath it, but neither is below the other. Without a single lowest element, ∨ is undefined. WordNet similarity software in this situation usually lists all candidate LCSs and takes the deepest.

Note: genuine lattices are common in semantics too: the semilattice of plural individuals in Logic for Formal Semantics 9.4, and the concept lattices that Formal Concept Analysis generates from an “object × feature” table, are examples.

4.4Property inheritance and exceptions

Property inheritance: a property is recorded only at the highest node to which it applies, and lower concepts acquire it automatically along ≤. “Breathes” is recorded at “animal”; dog and penguin both inherit it.

The trouble is exceptions: “birds fly”, but penguins do not. If “flies” is recorded at “bird”, penguin wrongly inherits it. The usual remedy is default inheritance: explicit information lower down can override defaults from above. This makes inference non-monotonic: knowing “Tweety is a bird” we conclude “Tweety flies”; learning “Tweety is a penguin”, we must retract it.

Exercise 3　Properties of relations

Decide whether each relation is reflexive, symmetric, antisymmetric, transitive: (a) antonymy (big/small); (b) synset co-membership “is in the same synset as”; (c) hyponymy (excluding self).

Answer
(a) Antonymy: symmetric, not reflexive, not transitive (big–small and small–big do not give big–big). (b) Same synset: reflexive, symmetric and transitive, an equivalence relation, and the synsets are exactly its equivalence classes. (c) Strict hyponymy: antisymmetric (in fact asymmetric), transitive and irreflexive, a strict partial order; adding reflexivity gives the partial order of 4.2.

Centrality and graph measures

Centrality and graph measures
Sources: standard definitions from introductory network science; values computed on this guide’s example graph

Which words are at the core of a lexical network? “Core” has at least three meanings: having the most friends, being closest to everyone, and lying on the paths others must take. The three meanings correspond to three kinds of centrality.

5.1Three centralities

Degree centrality
CD(v) = deg(v) / (n − 1): the proportion of direct neighbours.
Closeness centrality
CC(v) = (n − 1) / Σu d(v, u): the shorter the average distance to all nodes, the larger the value.
Betweenness centrality
CB(v) = Σs≠v≠t σst(v) / σst: σst is the number of shortest paths from s to t, and σst(v) the number of them passing through v.

5.2PageRank: centrality by random walk

PageRank (Brin & Page 1998): imagine someone walking randomly on a directed graph, at each step following an out-edge to a random neighbour with probability α and jumping to any node with probability 1 − α. The long-run probability of being at v is the PageRank of v:

PR(v) = (1 − α) / n + α Σu→v PR(u) / deg+(u)　　usually α = 0.85

A node pointed to by many important nodes has a high PageRank. Replacing “jump to any node” with “jump back to a few designated nodes” gives personalized PageRank: it measures how strongly each node is associated with those starting points. The graph-based word sense disambiguation of Chapter 11 uses this variant.

e.g.Start from a uniform PR = 0.25. Animal has three incoming edges: dog points only to animal (out-degree 1), cat points to animal and dog (out-degree 2), life points only to animal (out-degree 1); so in the first round PR(animal) = 0.15/4 + 0.85 × (0.25/1 + 0.25/2 + 0.25/1) = 0.0375 + 0.531 = 0.569. In the same round PR(cat) = 0.038: nothing points to cat, so it gets only the random-jump share. Iterating converges to dog 0.053, cat 0.038, animal 0.471, life 0.438 (summing to 1). Life has only one incoming edge yet ranks second: that edge comes from animal, which has the highest PR, and it is animal’s only out-edge.

5.3Clustering coefficient

Clustering coefficient C(v): the number of edges actually present among the neighbours of v, divided by the maximum possible number k(k − 1)/2 (k = deg(v).

C(v) = 2 T(v) / (k(k − 1))　　T(v): the number of triangles containing v

In the table on the previous page, the two neighbours of “patient” (doctor, hospital) are linked, so C = 1; the two neighbours of “school nurse” belong to different clusters, so C = 0. High clustering together with short average paths is the small-world signature of 3.4.

e.g.Take “student”: it has three neighbours (teacher, school, classroom), among which at most 3 × 2 / 2 = 3 edges are possible; two are present, teacher–school and school–classroom, while teacher and classroom are not linked, so C(student) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67. Triangles give the same result: the triangles containing student are {student, teacher, school} and {student, school, classroom}, so C = 2 × 2 / (3 × 2) = 0.67. The average clustering coefficient of the nine nodes is (0.67 + 0.33 + 0.67 + 1 + 0 + 0.33 + 1 + 0.67 + 1) / 9 ≈ 0.63.

5.4Degree distribution

Scale-free network: the degree distribution approximately follows a power law P(k) ∝ k−γ: most nodes have very low degree, while a few hubs have very high degree.

e.g.With γ = 2.5, the ratio of the probability of a degree-50 node to that of a degree-1 node is 50−2.5 ≈ 1 / 17,678: rare but not absent; in a network of ten thousand words one can expect a dozen or so hubs of degree 50 or more. In a random network with the same mean degree (Poisson distribution) the same ratio is about 10-50, effectively impossible. So once a few words (such as “person”, “thing”, “do”) are seen linked to a great many others, we know the lexical network was not wired at random. Taking logs gives log P(k) = −γ log k + constant, so a power law is a straight line on a log–log plot, the most common way to check for one.

Linguistic lens

Steyvers & Tenenbaum (2005) found that the degree distributions of word-association networks, WordNet and Roget’s Thesaurus are all close to power laws, and proposed a “semantic growth” model: new words tend to attach to words that already have many links, and these early-acquired, well-connected words are also responded to faster in naming and lexical decision tasks. Graph measures thus became a bridge between lexical structure and psycholinguistic data.
Exercise 4　Interpreting centrality

(a) In the table of 5.1, “school” has a higher degree than “school nurse”; why is its betweenness still slightly lower? (b) If an edge is added between “hospital” and “school”, which value drops sharply?

Answer

Chapter 6

Semantic networks: from Quillian to spreading activation

Semantic networks: from Quillian to spreading activation
Sources: Quillian (1968); Collins & Quillian (1969); Collins & Loftus (1975)

Word meanings were first drawn as networks not for computers but to explain human memory. Quillian (1968) proposed a model of semantic memory with nodes for concepts and links for relations; Collins and Quillian (1969) soon put it to experimental test.

6.1The hierarchical model and cognitive economy

Cognitive economy: “breathes” is stored once, at “animal”; canary and ostrich inherit it through hypernym links (4.4). “Ostriches can’t fly” is stored at ostrich itself, overriding the default above.

Sentence verification task: participants judge whether sentences are true while response times are recorded. The model predicts: the more hypernym links to traverse, the slower the response.

A canary can sing.property at “canary”: 0 levelsA canary can fly.up to “bird”: 1 levelA canary has skin.up to “animal”: 2 levels

The results roughly matched this order, and the hierarchical model became, for a while, the standard picture of semantic memory.

6.2Difficulties for the hierarchical model

Typicality effects
Rips, Shoben & Smith (1973) found that “a robin is a bird” is verified faster than “a penguin is a bird”, although both are one level from “bird”. In the hierarchical model all links at a level are equally long, so typicality cannot be explained.
Association strength
Conrad (1972) pointed out that “a canary can sing” may be fast simply because the two words often occur together, not because of where the property is stored.
Negative judgements
“A canary is a fish” is rejected quickly, though in the model the two are connected only via “animal” and should be slow.

6.3The spreading-activation model

Spreading activation (Collins & Loftus 1975): strict hierarchy is replaced by a general weighted graph. When a concept is processed, activation spreads outward along links, decaying with distance and time; the stronger and shorter the link, the faster the spread.

aj(t + 1) = δ · aj(t) + Σi wij · ai(t)　　δ < 1: decay; wij: link strength

The model explains semantic priming: in the lexical decision task of Meyer & Schvaneveldt (1971), seeing “doctor” first makes it faster to decide that “nurse” is a word, because “nurse” is already pre-activated. It is also the cognitive prototype of PageRank (Chapter 5) and graph-based disambiguation (Chapter 11): all three let some quantity flow along edges and see where it settles.

Exercise 5　Predicting response times

Using the hierarchical model of 6.1, rank the predicted response times of the following sentences (fast to slow), then explain how spreading activation would revise them: (a) An ostrich breathes. (b) An ostrich can’t fly. (c) An ostrich has feathers.

Answer
Hierarchical model: (b) 0 levels, fastest; (c) 1 level (to bird); (a) 2 levels (to animal), slowest. Spreading activation no longer just counts levels: “ostrich–can’t fly” is a frequently mentioned, strong association and may be even faster; “ostrich–has feathers”, if strongly associated, may also beat the level-based prediction. The difference between the two models is precisely the difference between structural distance and weighted distance (3.3).

Knowledge graphs and RDF triples

Knowledge graphs and RDF
Sources: W3C RDF 1.1 and SPARQL 1.1 specifications; on CWN as linked data see the Linguistic Linked Open Data literature

7.1Triples

Triple ⟨subject, predicate, object⟩: a labelled directed edge (1.4). RDF (Resource Description Framework) names subjects and predicates with globally unique IRIs; the object can be an IRI or a literal (string, number). A knowledge graph is a set of triples.

The triple ⟨Amin, knows, Hua⟩ corresponds to the predicate-logic formula knows(m, h): a knowledge graph amounts to a model with only binary predicates (see Logic for Formal Semantics 4.2). What about a ternary predicate such as “Amin gave Hua a book”? The usual solution is to introduce an event node e: ⟨e, agent, Amin⟩, ⟨e, theme, book⟩, ⟨e, recipient, Hua⟩.

7.2Queries as subgraph matching

SPARQL (SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language): a query is written as a small graph with variables, and the system finds every subgraph that matches it.

SELECT ?x WHERE {
　?x rdf:type ex:Person .
　?x ex:livesIn ex:Taipei .
}

The answer is ?x = ex:Amin. The property paths of SPARQL 1.1 let a query follow the same kind of edge any number of steps; for example ex:hypernym+ means “one or more hypernym steps”, i.e. the transitive closure of 4.2.

Note: lexical resources can also be published as linked data. CWN has been recast in the lemon lexicon model and published to the Linguistic Linked Open Data (LLOD) cloud; lemon, however, has no structure for “facets”, which needed a separate extension.

The structure of WordNet

The structure of WordNet
Sources: Miller (1995); Miller et al. (1990); Fellbaum (1998)

WordNet is the English lexical database built at Princeton under George A. Miller from 1985. It turned the psycholinguistic idea of semantic memory (Chapter 6) into a large-scale lexical network; it remains one of the most widely used lexical resources in natural language processing and the design prototype for wordnets in other languages, CWN included.

8.2Synsets

Synset: a row of the lexical matrix, i.e. a set of senses interchangeable in some contexts, with a gloss and example sentences. The nodes of WordNet are synsets, not words; a “sense” is the occurrence of a word form in a synset.

{car, auto, automobile, machine, motorcar}
a motor vehicle with four wheels; usually propelled by an internal combustion engine

WordNet 3.0 has about 118,000 synsets and 155,000 word forms. Nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs each form their own network, with few links between them.

8.4Semantic and lexical relations

Most relations link synsets (semantic relations between concepts); antonymy, however, links senses (lexical relations between word forms). large and small, big and little are antonyms, but large and little do not sound like a pair, although their meanings are opposed. Antonymy therefore cannot be defined at the synset level.

8.5The noun hierarchy

The noun network is the deepest part of WordNet: every noun synset can be traced up the hypernym links to the single root entity. The early design had 25 independent unique beginners, later unified under entity, making the noun hierarchy a rooted DAG (Chapter 2) and enabling the similarity computations of Chapter 10.

e.g.Take dog (dog.n.01) in Princeton WordNet 3.0: climbing via canine, it reaches entity in 13 steps; but it has a second hypernym, domestic animal, and by that route entity is only 8 steps away. Multiple inheritance gives the same node two depths (maximum 13, minimum 8), and every depth-based similarity (Wu-Palmer, Leacock-Chodorow in Chapter 10) must stipulate which one to use. The lowest common subsumer of dog and cat is carnivore, at depth 11.

Chapter 9

The Chinese Wordnet (CWN)

Chinese Wordnet
Sources: the CWN 2.0 website and CwnGraph documentation; Ahrens et al. (1998)

The Chinese Wordnet (CWN) was built by Chu-Ren Huang’s team at Academia Sinica and has since been developed by the LOPE lab of the Graduate Institute of Linguistics, National Taiwan University; the current version is CWN 2.0. Unlike many wordnets translated from the English WordNet, CWN starts from the Chinese lexicon itself: it first makes fine-grained sense distinctions, then builds lexical-semantic relations.

9.1Senses and facets

Meaning facet: CWN distinguishes two levels of polysemy (Ahrens et al. 1998). Senses are distinct meanings; facets are different aspects of one sense, related to each other and able to be talked about in the same sentence.

Sense distinctions
電腦 diànnǎo ‘computer’: sense 1 is a data-processing device, sense 2 is “knowledge of studying or operating computers”, and sense 3 is a metaphor for a person with great powers of calculation or memory.
Facet distinctions
Sense 1 is further divided into two facets: the computer’s function (programs, software) and its physical body (screen, keyboard, case). 這台電腦很重，但跑得很快 ‘this computer is heavy, but it runs fast’ talks about both facets at once.

Facets correspond to the dot types of Logic for Formal Semantics 8.5: “the book is heavy but interesting” is felicitous because the two predicates each select a different facet of the same sense.

9.2CwnGraph: CWN as a graph

The official Python package CwnGraph stores CWN as a typed graph: nodes are lemmas (lemma), senses (sense), facets (facet) and synsets (synset); edges carry relation labels and a direction (forward/reversed, the inverse relations of 2.1). 9.3Semantic relations in CWN

CWN’s relations largely follow WordNet: synonymy, antonymy, hypernymy, hyponymy, part and whole, and so on; two further points deserve attention.

Paranymy
paranymy: parallel members under one semantic classification, such as 春、夏、秋、冬 ‘spring, summer, autumn, winter’ or 東、西、南、北 ‘east, west, south, north’. They are neither synonyms nor hyponyms but share a classificatory frame.
Marked on senses
Lexical relations such as paranymy are marked between individual senses in CWN, not between whole synsets; this echoes the observation of 8.4 that lexical relations cannot be defined at the synset level.

Linguistic lens

Paranymy highlights a feature of the Chinese lexicon: large parallel word groups built on shared morphemes, such as 春天、夏天、秋天、冬天 ‘spring, summer, autumn, winter’ or 父親、母親 ‘father, mother’. The English WordNet can only hang them individually under a common hypernym as sister nodes; CWN marks the parallel relation explicitly. For the graph, this adds a layer of edges between sister nodes, changing the clustering coefficient (5.3) and the distances between nodes.

Semantic similarity

Semantic similarity
Sources: Leacock & Chodorow (1998); Wu & Palmer (1994); Resnik (1995); Jiang & Conrath (1997); Lin (1998); Budanitsky & Hirst (2006)

With the hypernym DAG (Chapter 2) and the lowest common subsumer (4.3), “how alike are two senses” can be turned into a number. All values in this chapter are computed on the illustrative wordnet of Chapter 13, so readers can verify each one in the browser.

Path similarity
simpath(a, b) = 1 / (d + 1), where d is the shortest-path length between the two in the hypernym graph.
Leacock–Chodorow
simlch = −log((d + 1) / 2D), where D is the maximum depth of the whole hierarchy, used to normalise path length.
Wu–Palmer
simwup = 2 · depth(LCS) / (depth(a) + depth(b)): the deeper the LCS, the more specific what the two share.

10.2Information-based measures

Information content: IC(c) = −log P(c). P(c) is the probability that a noun drawn at random from a corpus belongs to c or to any concept below c. The higher the concept, the more common it is and the lower its IC; the root has IC 0.

simres(a, b) = IC(LCS)
simlin(a, b) = 2 · IC(LCS) / (IC(a) + IC(b))
distjcn(a, b) = IC(a) + IC(b) − 2 · IC(LCS)　(similarity is its reciprocal)

Resnik looks only at how much information is shared; Lin and Jiang–Conrath also take each sense’s own information into account: the larger the share of the whole that is shared, the more similar.

10.3Similarity and relatedness

Taxonomic measures capture only similarity: things of the same kind. “Dog” and “bone”, “doctor” and “hospital” are highly related (relatedness) yet far apart in the hypernym graph. Measuring relatedness requires other edges (part–whole, glosses, co-occurrence) or the vector methods of Chapter 12. Budanitsky & Hirst (2006) compared WordNet measures against human ratings and application performance and found that no measure is best in every setting.

Linguistic lens

Information content requires a sense-tagged corpus: one must know which sense of 花 huā each occurrence is in order to credit the count to the right node. English commonly uses SemCor; for Chinese there is the Chinese Wordnet SemCor dataset, tagged with CWN 2.0 senses, which manually annotates high-frequency polysemous words with more than 10 senses in CWN. Without a tagged corpus, the usual fallback is to divide a word form’s count evenly among all its senses.

Chapter 11

Word sense disambiguation

Word sense disambiguation
Sources: Lesk (1986); Kilgarriff & Rosenzweig (2000); Agirre & Soroa (2009); Navigli & Lapata (2010)

Word sense disambiguation (WSD): given a polysemous word in context, decide which sense in the wordnet it has. The wordnet supplies candidate senses with their glosses and relations; the disambiguation method decides how to use that information.

11.1The Lesk algorithm: matching glosses

Simplified Lesk: for each candidate sense of the target word, count how many words its gloss (and examples) share with the context, and pick the sense with the most overlap.

Context: 她 / 把 / 這個 / 月 / 的 / 薪水 / 都 / 花 / 在 / 買 / 書 / 上　‘she spent this month’s salary on books’Content words: 月 ‘month’, 薪水 ‘salary’, 買 ‘buy’, 書 ‘book’
Candidate senseGloss (illustrative)OverlapScore
花₁the reproductive organ of a plant, often brightly coloured—0
花₂to use up money or time in order to buy things or do somethingbuy1

花₂ wins. Chinese must first be segmented before overlap can be counted; when glosses are short the overlap is often zero, so extended Lesk (Banerjee & Pedersen 2002) also compares the glosses of related synsets, in effect widening the comparison along the edges of the wordnet.

11.2Graph-based disambiguation: personalized PageRank

Personalized PageRank WSD (Agirre & Soroa 2009): treat the whole wordnet as a graph, concentrate the “jump” probability on the senses of the context words (5.2), and run PageRank; the highest-scoring candidate sense of the target word is the answer.

The method needs no tagged corpus, only the graph structure; its intuition is that of spreading activation (6.3): the context activates a set of nodes, activation flows through the wordnet, and the most visited sense wins. Navigli & Lapata (2010) systematically compared graph connectivity measures (degree, PageRank, betweenness and others) for disambiguation.

Exercise 7　Weaknesses of Lesk

Use simplified Lesk on 花 in 他花了三個小時寫報告 ‘he spent three hours writing the report’. With the glosses in the table, what is the result? What if the gloss of 花₂ is changed to just “use up”?

Answer
Context content words: 三 ‘three’, 小時 ‘hour’, 寫 ‘write’, 報告 ‘report’. The gloss of 花₂ contains 時間 ‘time’, but the context has 小時 ‘hour’, so there is no literal overlap; both senses score 0 and no decision is possible. If the gloss shrinks to “use up”, there is no overlap at all. Simplified Lesk is thus very sensitive to the wording of glosses; extended Lesk or graph methods can connect the two through the hypernym edge 小時 ‘hour’ → 時間 ‘time’.

Chapter 12

Graph embeddings

Graph embeddings
Sources: Perozzi et al. (2014); Grover & Leskovec (2016); Bordes et al. (2013); Nickel & Kiela (2017); Faruqui et al. (2015)

The similarities of Chapter 10 can only be computed pair by pair on the graph. Graph embeddings map each node to a k-dimensional vector so that nodes close in the graph are close in vector space; similarity then becomes the cosine of vectors, and the vectors can be fed straight into machine-learning models.

12.1Random walks plus a language model

DeepWalk (Perozzi et al. 2014): run many random walks from every node, treat each walk as a “sentence”, and learn node vectors with word2vec’s skip-gram: nodes that often appear in the same walk get similar vectors. This is the graph version of the distributional idea that words in similar contexts have similar meanings.

node2vec (Grover & Leskovec 2016): the walk becomes a biased second-order walk. Having just stepped from t to v, the weight of returning to t is 1/p, of moving to a neighbour of t is 1, and of moving farther away is 1/q.

Tuning p and q decides whether the vectors reflect “members of the same cluster” or nodes that “play similar structural roles” (such as the hubs of two different word clusters).

12.2Knowledge-graph embeddings and hyperbolic space

TransE (Bordes et al. 2013): relations are vectors too, and a triple ⟨h, r, t⟩ is required to satisfy h + r ≈ t; e.g. dog + hypernym ≈ mammal. It handles one-to-one relations well but struggles with one-to-many (a hypernym with many hyponyms).

The number of nodes in a tree grows exponentially with depth, more than Euclidean space can accommodate; the volume of hyperbolic space also grows exponentially with radius. The Poincaré embeddings of Nickel & Kiela (2017) can therefore preserve the WordNet noun hierarchy in very few dimensions: higher concepts lie near the centre, more specific ones near the edge.

Linguistic lens

Corpus vectors and wordnets complement each other. In retrofitting (Faruqui et al. 2015), word vectors are first trained on a corpus, then each word’s vector is pulled towards its neighbours in WordNet: distributional information provides coverage, the wordnet provides hand-curated relations. For Chinese, corpus vectors struggle to separate 花₁ ‘flower’ from 花₂ ‘spend’, and the sense nodes of CWN can supply exactly that layer.

Exercise 8　Same path, different answers

Compare three pairs: dog–cat, dog–whale, penguin–sparrow. (a) Which measures give all three the same score? (b) Which pair do Resnik and Lin each judge most similar, and why do they differ?

Answer
(a) All three have path length 2 and an LCS at depth 5, so path similarity, Leacock–Chodorow and Wu–Palmer coincide (0.333, 1.386, 0.833). (b) Resnik looks only at IC(LCS): “bird” is rarer than “mammal” and has higher IC (2.649 vs 1.629), so penguin–sparrow is most similar. Lin normalises by the two senses’ own IC: penguin and sparrow are themselves rare (IC about 4.9 and 4.5), so the shared part is only a little over half; dog and cat are common (IC about 2.3 and 2.6), giving the highest shared proportion (0.653), so dog–cat is most similar.
Exercise 9　Multiple inheritance

(a) Set A to “book” and B to “computer”: what is the LCS? (b) What if B is “information”? (c) If the edge “book → information” is removed, how does the result of (b) change?

Answer
(a) Artifact: book → artifact ← tool ← computer, path length 3. (b) Information itself: book is a direct hyponym of information, path length 1, Wu–Palmer = 2 × 3 / (4 + 3) ≈ 0.857. (c) Book is left with the single hypernym chain through “artifact”, and its LCS with information falls back to “entity”; the path length becomes 4 (book → artifact → object → entity ← abstraction ← information) and Resnik is 0. A single edge can change the similarity drastically: this is something to check carefully when computing similarity with a wordnet.
Exercise 10　Similar is not related

In the browser, compare “wheel–car” with “bicycle–car”. Which pair is more similar by the taxonomic measures? Does this match intuition?

Answer
The LCS of bicycle–car is “vehicle” (path length 2) and that of wheel–car is “artifact” (path length 3), so the taxonomic measures judge bicycle and car more similar. This fits the definition of similarity (same kind), but wheel and car stand in a part–whole relation and are very closely related; the hypernym path cannot see that edge (10.3).
